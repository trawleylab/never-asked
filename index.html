The Never-Asked: Understanding the Gap in Emotional Health Support for People Living with Diabetes in Australia

Kathleen Hare1 · Sybil McAuley2 · Steven Trawley1,2

1 The Cairnmillar Institute, Hawthorn East, VIC
2 School of Public Health and Preventive Medicine, Monash University, Melbourne, VIC

Three in four Australians with diabetes have never been asked about their emotional health.

The people who need it most are no more likely to report being asked.

Key findings

  • 797 of 1,092 adults (73%) had never been asked about their emotional health by a diabetes health professional.
  • 42% screened positive for diabetes distress (PAID-5 ≥ 8) — 44% of those asked and 41% of those never asked (p = 0.335). Enquiry is not reaching those who need it most.
  • Of the 459 people with elevated distress, 328 had never been asked and only 11.5% were receiving psychological support.
  • Where asking happened it was almost always informal: 268 of 295 (90%); only 6.8% were asked using a questionnaire.
  • The gap reaches everyone. In the adjusted model there was no difference by diabetes type, insulin use, who leads the care, gender, diabetes duration or HbA1c. People not using CGM had nearly twice the odds of never being asked (OR 1.93, 95% CI 1.19–3.12).

Method

National online cross-sectional survey of Australian adults with self-reported diabetes, recruited through a research panel and paid social media. Of 1,300 responses, 1,092 were eligible. Participants were asked: “Has your diabetes healthcare professional ever asked you about your emotional health related to living with diabetes?” Diabetes distress was measured with the PAID-5. A pre-specified multivariable logistic regression identified predictors of never being asked; 533 free-text responses were analysed thematically.

In their own words

“Care is very black and white. If my levels are stable, no further questions are asked” 31-year-old with type 2 diabetes, diagnosed 3 years
“I wish they did coz it gets you down a lot of the time living with this” 29-year-old with type 1 diabetes, diagnosed 1 year
“It made me feel that my overall well-being, not just my blood sugar, was being taken into account” 65-year-old with type 1 diabetes, diagnosed 17 years

Limitations

Cross-sectional and self-reported; “ever asked” relies on recall and counts a single past occasion. Convenience sample recruited through a paid panel and social media, so estimates may not generalise; type 1 diabetes is over-represented relative to the national population.
